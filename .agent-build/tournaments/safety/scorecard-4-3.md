## Scores

- **Requirements coverage — 4.5/10.** Sections 4–17 are an unusually complete dynamic-assessment sub-spec, but §2 explicitly limits scope to runtime candidacy, probing, runtime credentials, and runtime evidence; PLAN P2 also requires SSH/provider credential lifecycle, static/acquisition/container controls, provider prompt-injection defenses, all network classes and optional hosted services, general audit/finding governance, and product-wide redaction and residual-risk acceptance.
- **Assets, attacker model, and trust boundaries — 7/10.** §3 identifies the host, provider/SSH assets, customer data, hostile repository/build/browser/scanner inputs, compromised processes, and operator mistakes, but it does not map actor capabilities and controls across source acquisition, host-side static analyzers, provider compartments, the local UI/API session, optional services, reporting, packaging, or deletion.
- **Prohibited actions and authorization — 8/10.** The P0–P3 taxonomy and `PX-PROHIBITED` list in §6 are specific, conservative, and enforceable at the browser interceptor, yet authority outside dynamic probes remains unspecified and the authorization contract cannot be operated in its stated order.
- **Container, nested-runtime, and source-isolation controls — 6/10.** §§5, 8, 9, and DYN-01/08/11/16 require a pinned VM, rootless Docker, cgroups, firewalling, non-root browser, safe mounts, quotas, and immutable source, but most Compose/Docker rejection behavior is delegated to “architecture section 10.2” (§5.3) and the host-side acquisition/static-analyzer containers are not reviewed or tested.
- **Credential lifecycle and secret handling — 6/10.** §§4, 5.7, 7.1, 11, and DYN-05/13 strongly constrain sandbox target credentials, session state, one-use handles, recipient scope, persistence, and package leakage, but they do not specify end-to-end SSH, provider-home/token, optional-service, or encryption-secret lifecycles, and only test rather than review the frozen X25519/HKDF/AES-GCM VM-envelope profile.
- **Network egress and hosted-service controls — 5/10.** Runtime IPv4/IPv6/DNS/LAN/metadata/public denial and exact-origin interception are strong (§§5.4–5.6, 8, DYN-02/11), but provider inference, Git acquisition, build acquisition, tool updates, and optional services receive only consent or residual-risk language; there is no complete state machine, approval-to-egress receipt contract, revocation behavior, or no-silent-fallback suite for those architecture network classes.
- **Safe dynamic testing and emergency control — 8/10.** §§6–9 and 13 define excellent read-only safety classes, budgets, stop triggers, coverage reconciliation, and destructive-action denial; the score is capped because the gate/build ordering is impossible, state-changing `GET` behavior cannot be established before first execution, and stop/destroy has no deadline/acknowledgement/escalation contract.
- **Prompt injection and untrusted-content defense — 4/10.** Prompt injection is merely declared untrusted in §3.2 and DOM text is declared non-authoritative in §8.1; there is no provider context-construction rule, tool/capability allowlist, approval-spoofing defense, provider credential boundary, audit event, or Codex/Claude hostile-prompt fixture.
- **Evidence integrity, privacy, redaction, and packaging — 7/10.** §§10–12 provide a precise source-to-request lineage, bounded metadata, screenshot corroboration rule, data map, provenance states, coverage semantics, and final secret scanning, but the spec only governs dynamic evidence and relies on architecture rather than reviewing product-wide evidence admission, active-content rendering, package staging, manifest/checksum integrity, retention, deletion, and post-delivery leakage.
- **Failure, incident response, and auditability — 6/10.** §13 gives concrete incident triggers, containment order, re-attestation, operator notice, non-resumption, and release consequences, but `INTERRUPTED_BY_SAFETY` (§13.2) is not a frozen architecture state, no typed security audit-event catalog exists, and cleanup failure lacks bounded escalation and a precise operator recovery state.
- **Finding governance and compliance-language safety — 5/10.** §2 correctly prohibits certification/compliance/legal-applicability claims and §12 prevents false clean results, but “low-confidence finding,” “Critical product incident,” and “High-signal safety event” are used without rules for technical severity, business priority, confidence, validation state, CVSS sufficiency, dispute/supersession, reviewer independence, or package/release effects.
- **Verification, operational usability, and buildability — 6/10.** DYN-01–16 are concrete and ownership in §15 maps well to P5 packages, but the suite validates only the dynamic slice, new version-`1.0.0` DTOs and a new attempt state conflict with frozen contracts, and a non-specialist operator receives no workable flow for discovering safe routes, authoring login templates, provisioning role/tenant fixtures, resolving ambiguous account mappings, or recovering from reauthorization.

## Overall

**6.0/10.** Equal-weight average. Contender 4 is the strongest kind of reusable dynamic-runtime safety module: conservative, traceable, and candid about passive-test limits. It is not an implementation-ready P2 product safety specification. Operationally, its central gate cannot complete in the stated sequence, and most non-runtime risk remains governed only by the architecture or not at all.

## Strongest objection

**Must-fix — the runtime authorization/gate is temporally impossible and creates pressure for an unsafe bypass.** Section 5 says the gate runs “before any target image pull, build, [or] container creation” (§5, lines 182–183), but gate condition 6 requires “build acquisition [to be] finished and disconnected” (lines 203–206). Authorization must already bind `compiledPlanDigest`, internal origins, destinations, and credential profiles (§4, lines 116–161), while a changed image digest invalidates authorization even though `RuntimeTestAuthorization` has no image-digest field and the architecture produces image digests and internal origins only after `vm.build` and `vm.start`. The frozen architecture deliberately sequences `vm.preflight → vm.create → vm.stageSnapshot → vm.compile → vm.acquireBuildInputs → vm.build → vm.start → vm.probe`; this contender collapses those distinct trust decisions into one pre-build “gate.”

An implementation therefore has only three bad choices: build before the spec permits, fabricate unavailable digests/origins, or add an unplanned bypass/reapproval path. This is both a safety defect and a severe operator-usability defect: legitimate runs deadlock, while operators are incentivized to approve opaque future behavior. It must be replaced by explicit phase gates and separately scoped approvals before implementation.

## Other findings

1. **Must-fix — this is a dynamic-testing spec, not the required product safety spec (§2, lines 40–49).** Its own scope excludes the majority of PLAN P2: source acquisition and local-path attacks, host-side analyzer isolation, provider/SSH credentials, provider prompt injection, optional hosted services, product-wide evidence/report/package handling, security finding governance, and full audit events. A malicious repository can compromise an earlier provider/analyzer/build boundary before any excellent probe control executes.

2. **Must-fix — prompt injection has no enforceable control or test (§3.2 line 80; §8.1 lines 334–335).** Saying injected text is untrusted does not specify what Codex or Claude receives, which tools it can invoke, whether repository instructions/config/MCP can alter task policy, how approval spoofing is rejected, or how attempts are audited. DYN-01–16 never seed injection through source files, filenames, scanner output, target pages, or provider responses.

3. **Must-fix — frozen contract compatibility is broken (§4 lines 112–155; §13.2 lines 533–534).** The contender creates two “canonical” `1.0.0` records while permitting field-name variance, rather than supplying a versioned additive contract or mapping to the frozen `CapabilityResult`, `Approval`, `SecretHandle`, host operations, OpenAPI, and persistence model. It also invents `INTERRUPTED_BY_SAFETY`, which is absent from the architecture state vocabulary. P4/P5 cannot implement this without an unplanned contract migration.

4. **Must-fix — provider, Git, build, and optional-service egress are disclosure-only (§3.3, §4, §11.1).** The spec candidly says approved provider/build destinations remain exfiltration channels, but it does not bind actual requests and bytes to an approval digest, define DNS/IP/redirect enforcement for each architecture network class, specify expiry/revocation during an in-flight transfer, or make residual acceptance accountable. “Approved destination” is not equivalent to approved data.

5. **Must-fix — static hostile-execution boundaries are absent (entire spec).** The architecture feeds malicious repository bytes to acquisition and multiple host-side scanner/parser containers. This contender does not freeze UID/rootfs/mount/capability/seccomp/network/output/control-socket rules for those workers and has no parser-compromise, cross-run read, helper-socket, output-flood, or orphan-cleanup fixture. Dynamic VM hardening does not cover this earlier attack path.

6. **Must-fix — the read-only method model cannot prove absence of side effects (§6 lines 234–255).** `GET`, `HEAD`, and `OPTIONS` are syntactically allowed while state-changing `GET` is prohibited, but there is no preflight proof or post-request fixture-state comparison that distinguishes them before the first request. A malicious or defective target can mutate its local database, enqueue internal work, or lock an account on `GET`; external egress denial reduces but does not eliminate the consequence. Scope claims and evidence status need a safe detection/reconciliation rule.

7. **Must-fix — residual risk is disclosed but never accepted (§16).** The list is candid, especially for hypervisor/browser zero-days, approved-channel exfiltration, sandbox/production drift, incomplete account matrices, redaction misses, and narrow tenant checks. However, there is no residual-risk record with owner, affected asset/control, likelihood/impact, compensating control, acceptance authority, expiry/review date, customer disclosure, or release threshold. PLAN P2 explicitly requires residual-risk acceptance, not merely prose.

8. **Must-fix — security finding governance is underspecified (§7.2 line 310; §13.2 lines 552–555; §15.1 line 605).** “Low-confidence,” “Critical,” “High-signal,” and independent review drive behavior without schema or transition rules. Define separate technical severity, business priority, confidence, validation state, CVSS prerequisites, reviewer independence, dispute/invalidation/supersession, and exactly which unresolved states block release or affect decision confidence.

9. **Should-fix — the operator workflow assumes expert setup despite the product’s calm, guided non-specialist requirement (§§4 and 7).** The operator must understand compiled-plan/control-plan digests, internal origins, login templates, principal/role/tenant mappings, safe fixture object IDs, data classes, route allowlists, and reduced ZAP semantics. The spec does not say how these are discovered, validated, previewed, corrected, or reapproved; SSO/MFA-only applications and ambiguous tenants simply fall into implicit dead ends. Define a staged preview, readiness checklist, per-account verification, actionable blocked reasons, and safe reset/reapproval flow.

10. **Should-fix — incident stop is not operationally bounded (§13.2).** The sequence names `stop`, `destroy`, and host emergency stop but no acknowledgement states, deadlines, retries, UI availability when the API/broker is impaired, reconciliation after restart, or exact outcome when residue remains. Reuse the architecture’s deadline-bearing `vm.stop`, `vm.emergencyStop`, `vm.destroy`, `CleanupResult`, and reconciliation contracts rather than leaving P5 to invent escalation.

11. **Should-fix — internal VM lateral movement is only residualized (§3.2, §8.1, §16).** A compromised browser, target, ZAP process, or worker daemon is in the threat model, yet the spec does not define VM-internal ACLs separating target services, probe data plane, broker control plane, and evidence collection. A browser zero-day is listed as residual risk before proving it cannot reach broker control or another service outside the signed origin plan.

## Concrete fixes

1. Replace the monolithic gate with a phase ledger matching the frozen host protocol: preflight and policy compilation before acquisition; a separately disclosed build-acquisition approval; build with no sandbox credential; image-digest verification; start to discover broker-owned internal origins; then a probe authorization bound to image/service/origin/control-plan/probe/account digests. Any change re-enters only the affected gate.
2. Expand the safety spec beyond dynamic runtime. Add normative control and test matrices for local/SSH acquisition, host-side static analyzers, provider compartments, local UI/API authorization, reporting/packaging, retention/deletion, and every architecture network class.
3. Add a provider prompt-injection contract: fixed task/context construction, repository instructions/config as quoted data, release-owned tool allowlists, no model authority over approval/policy/destinations/secrets/helper operations, schema/policy validation of proposals, safe audit events, and equivalent hostile fixtures for Codex and Claude.
4. Reconcile every new field and state with frozen architecture. Prefer additive `rak-contract/1.1.0` schemas and explicit mappings to `Approval`, `CapabilityResult`, `SecretHandle`, host/broker operations, DB tables, OpenAPI, SSE, and reducers; replace field-name latitude with exact canonical names.
5. Define and verify source/acquisition/static-analyzer workload profiles: numeric UID, read-only root and snapshot, isolated writable outbox, no network, dropped capabilities, no control sockets, bounded resources/output, locked parsers, current-fence receipts, and adversarial cleanup/reconciliation.
6. Add a complete egress authorization and audit contract per network class, binding destination resolution, methods, data categories/paths, recipient, credentials, attempt/fence, bytes/requests, redirects, policy digest, expiry/revocation, receipt, and no-silent-fallback behavior. Require accountable residual acceptance where an approved build/provider channel can still exfiltrate source.
7. Add side-effect detection for permitted requests: operator-confirmed safe-route provenance, disposable fixture snapshot/checksum before and after each probe group, internal queue/outbox sentinels, account-lockout indicators, and automatic `partial`/incident handling when a supposedly read-only route changes state.
8. Freeze typed audit, finding, review, and residual-risk schemas and their operator flows. Include event triggers and safe payloads, severity/confidence/validation transitions, reviewer eligibility and disagreement, risk owner/expiry, release effects, plain-language disclosures, and reauthorization/recovery steps.
9. Turn incident response into a timed state machine using the existing stop/emergency-stop/destroy/reconcile primitives, including acknowledgements, stale-heartbeat escalation, restart reconciliation, cleanup residue, operator-visible next actions, and whether static packaging may continue after each incident class.

## Verdict

**Reject.** It clears the bar as a high-quality dynamic-runtime safety module and should be grafted into the synthesized spec. It misses the P2 ship bar because its own scope omits most product trust boundaries, its provider/prompt/static-worker risks remain uncontrolled, its residual risks lack acceptance governance, and its central gate cannot execute in the stated order.
