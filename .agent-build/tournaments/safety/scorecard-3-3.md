## Scores

- **Threat model, assets, and trust boundaries — 6/10.** Sections 2.1–2.2 inventory sensitive assets and correctly make repository instructions, provider output, scanner output, target pages, and imported artifacts untrusted, but §2.3's “Priority threats” is almost entirely an evidence/package threat model: it never models hostile Dockerfiles/Compose, worker/container escape, source mutation, local-API/session attack, target-to-probe attack, production-endpoint confusion, or abuse of the authenticated host helper.
- **Safe defaults, prohibited actions, and hostile-runtime isolation — 2/10.** The evidence and release paths fail closed, but the spec never states the runtime invariants frozen in architecture §§10.2–10.4: no host Docker socket, disposable Lima VM, broker-only rootless Docker, no arbitrary helper command, rejected Compose capabilities/mounts/namespaces, read-only roots, resource limits, default-deny firewall, internal-only target network, and residue-aware teardown. Merely naming the “pinned worker image” and “runtime broker” as trusted in §2.2 does not tell P4/P5 what to enforce or test.
- **Credential lifecycle, network egress, and hosted-service consent — 4/10.** Sections 3.2 and 5.1 give good purpose limitation, opaque one-use handles, exact destination/data disclosure, and no silent upload, but omit the SSH/provider/sandbox credential lifecycle and architecture's separate network classes, approval semantics, redirect/DNS/tunnel controls, destination/method/byte constraints, envelope replay/recipient checks, audit receipts, revocation timing, and provider-home cleanup default. “Stop further egress” in §10.5 has no defined enforcement path.
- **Evidence integrity, privacy, redaction, and safe rendering — 9/10.** Sections 4–8 are unusually complete: per-run blob/occurrence separation, fenced admission, truncation honesty, detector layers, typed non-correlating redaction, metadata-safe screenshot handling, attachment-only active content, typed report ASTs, immutable staged revisions, fresh-process ZIP validation, and authenticated-digest caveats are specific and adversarially testable. Minor feasibility gaps remain around how raw secret-bearing digests are suppressed consistently and how passphrases are transported without argv/environment exposure.
- **Safe dynamic testing, authorization, and prompt-injection defense — 2/10.** The spec does not define runtime-test safety classes, permitted HTTP methods/actions, destructive-action denial, authorization evidence, production-like endpoint sentinels, sandbox-only credential checks, or the capability gate that decides whether Playwright may run. It labels repository “instructions” and model output untrusted (§2.2) and prevents them from directly admitting evidence, but provides no prompt-injection controls for provider context, repository instruction suppression, tool/credential isolation, deceptive approval text, or malicious model/tool-call fixtures.
- **Incident stop, containment, recovery, and deletion — 5/10.** Sections 9–10 are concrete for leaks, tampered packages, parser failure, ENOSPC, and two-phase deletion, including the honest “delivery cannot be recalled” language. They do not define an operator emergency-stop control, stop ordering and deadlines, firewall cut-off, `vm.emergencyStop`, orphan reconciliation, cleanup-residue handling, safe static continuation, or recovery from a compromised target/runtime/helper—the incidents most likely while executing hostile code.
- **Auditability, finding severity/confidence, and compliance language — 5/10.** Section 3.3 properly forbids inferred applicability and unsupported compliance/certification claims, and §§4/7 preserve provenance and confidence effects. However, P2 explicitly requires audit events and severity/confidence/validation rules; the spec supplies neither an event taxonomy/required fields nor severity and confidence scales, CVSS sufficiency rules, validation-state transitions, downgrade/escalation rules, or treatment of disputed High/Critical findings.
- **Control enforceability, verification, and architecture fit — 6/10.** Sections 11–12 provide excellent negative fixtures and release blockers for evidence, rendering, packaging, retention, and incidents, and §14 assigns owners. The gates reveal the missing scope: there is no sandbox/egress/runtime-test/prompt-injection/emergency-stop gate, even though frozen architecture §17 already requires those fixtures. P4/P5 would have to invent security-critical behavior, so the contender does not satisfy P2's “every trust boundary and prohibited action” completion bar.
- **Operational usability, plain-language controls, and review burden — 4/10.** Leak/no-recall and retained-data messages are understandable, but most requirements are implementation jargon with no operator-visible control model, status/reason vocabulary, stop/recovery choices, or actionable denial text. Section 7.2 makes four reviews blockers and requires inspection of “every artifact classified customer-confidential or derived from secret-suspected input” without defining artifact granularity, queues, batch review, delta re-review, delegation, time bounds, or what remains blocked; for a confidential repository this can amount to reviewing nearly the whole package and makes predictable completion doubtful.
- **Residual-risk honesty — 8/10.** Section 13 accurately limits scanners, allowlists, detached digests, age, redaction, deletion, and recipient tooling, and requires decision-material limitations in customer reports. It is still incomplete because it does not disclose the residual risk created by provider prompt injection while provider credentials are present, accidental production targeting during dynamic tests, unverified runtime isolation/cleanup on the four-host matrix, or the operational risk that required human review may be incomplete or inconsistent.

## Overall

**5.1/10 (equal-weight average).** This is a strong evidence-integrity and deliverable-safety sub-spec, but only a partial safety specification for the product described in the brief and frozen architecture. It misses several P2-mandated domains that are both release-critical and operator-facing.

## Strongest objection

**Must-fix:** Sections 2, 10, 11, and 12 do not specify or gate the hostile-runtime safety path at all. The threat table (§2.3) jumps from untrusted Docker/Compose input (§2.2) to redaction/rendering/package threats; the incident section only says to “stop relevant … runtime” (§10.1) without a stop protocol; and the acceptance/release gates contain no VM isolation, Compose-policy, egress, dynamic-authorization, secret-envelope, emergency-stop, or orphan-cleanup tests. This leaves P4/P5 to invent the controls around the only feature that deliberately executes customer-controlled code, creating the highest-probability source of security rework and release failure.

## Other findings

1. **Must-fix — safe dynamic testing is undefined (§§2.3, 11, 12).** There is no classification of passive/read-only/state-changing/destructive tests, no method/action allowlist, no sandbox-only credential proof, no production-like endpoint sentinel, and no rule for converting a rejected control to `blocked` rather than silently relaxing it. This fails brief AC-4 and the P2 authorization requirement.
2. **Must-fix — prompt injection is named but not controlled (§2.2).** “Repository … instructions” and provider/model output are untrusted, yet the only invariant is that they cannot directly create admitted evidence. Nothing constrains what repository content reaches a provider, which instructions are ignored, which tools the provider may invoke, whether hostile text can forge operator approval, or how provider-session credentials remain out of reach. Rendering tests in §11.3 do not test agent manipulation.
3. **Must-fix — egress and approval controls are disclosure-only (§§3.2, 10.5).** Exact destination/data disclosure is valuable, but the spec does not bind approval to destination, method, data category, credential, attempt, expiry, and policy digest; nor does it require network enforcement or audit receipts. The architecture's distinct `provider-inference`, `git-acquisition`, `build-acquisition`, `target-runtime`, and `optional-service` classes are not reviewed.
4. **Must-fix — emergency stop and runtime recovery are not operable (§10).** There is no always-available stop action, acknowledgement state, deadline/escalation sequence, egress cut, secret revocation order, target/VM destroy, residue status, reconciliation after server/helper restart, or rule for preserving safely closed receipts. “Stop relevant … activity” is not an implementable incident contract.
5. **Must-fix — audit/severity/confidence contracts are absent (§§4, 7, 10).** The spec discusses audit metadata and High/Critical review, but never defines security event types, actors, approval/egress/secret/stop events, severity calculation, technical-versus-business priority, confidence scale, validation states, dispute handling, or which transitions block release. This makes independent review and customer prioritization inconsistent.
6. **Must-fix — the review gate is operationally unbounded (§7.2).** Requiring manual inspection of every customer-confidential or secret-suspected-derived artifact can encompass most evidence. No review inventory, progress count, role/independence proof, batching, sampled-versus-mandatory rule, delta invalidation, SLA, or safe “cannot complete review” outcome is given. The likely result is review fatigue or an indefinitely blocked package.
7. **Should-fix — retention defaults preserve sensitive state indefinitely (§9.1).** Provider homes and packages persist “until explicit” cleanup, with no default reminder, engagement-close workflow, inactivity warning, or plain-language exposure summary. The residual-risk section admits the issue, but safe operator defaults should reduce reliance on memory.
8. **Should-fix — secret-digest and passphrase mechanisms need reconciliation (§§4.1, 5.3, 8.5).** Admission requires hashing every received blob, while `sourceDigestRetained` may be false for secret-bearing sources without explaining whether the CAS blob still exists or how references work. Likewise, “protected channel” for generated age passphrases is not tied to a concrete broker/stdin/FD/UI handoff and recovery behavior.

## Concrete fixes

1. Add a hostile-execution section that normatively adopts the frozen helper/VM boundaries: fixed authenticated typed operations only; no host socket, generic exec, passthrough Compose, host network/ports, arbitrary mounts or destinations; disposable native-architecture Lima VM; rootless broker-only Docker; compile-and-reject policy; read-only/non-root/resource-bounded services; default-deny runtime egress; and residue-aware teardown.
2. Add a runtime-control authorization matrix with safety classes, allowed methods/actions, prohibited destructive and external effects, sandbox-credential provenance, target-origin/production-sentinel checks, required approvals, evidence outputs, and exact `blocked`/`partial` outcomes when a control cannot run safely.
3. Add a prompt-injection control table spanning repository instructions, model context construction, provider tool permissions, approval spoofing, URLs/content returned by targets, provider credential exposure, and output admission. Add hostile prompt fixtures for both Codex and Claude Code and a release blocker for any policy bypass.
4. Define the egress state machine and approval binding using the architecture DTO: network class, exact destination/method, data categories, recipient, credential handle, run/attempt, expiry/revocation, bytes/requests, redirect/DNS behavior, policy digest, and audit receipt. State the safe default and visible blocked reason for every class.
5. Specify one operator emergency-stop action and its deterministic sequence: revoke approvals/secrets, cut target/build/optional egress, cancel provider/helper jobs, issue bounded VM stop then `vm.emergencyStop`/destroy, close or quarantine outboxes, record cleanup residue, reconcile on restart, and continue only safe static work after explicit recovery.
6. Add required security audit-event, severity, confidence, and validation-state schemas, including separate technical severity/business priority, CVSS only when inputs suffice, dispute/invalidation semantics, redacted fields, retention, and release-blocking transitions.
7. Turn the review requirement into a bounded review workflow: exact review queue, mandatory artifact categories, counts and progress, safe grouped review, screenshot-at-readable-zoom requirement, independence identity, objections, delta invalidation after changes, timeout/unavailable outcome, and operator-facing reason/action text. Narrow “every customer-confidential artifact” to a justified risk-based set or explicitly budget and paginate it.
8. Add operator-facing plain-language copy requirements for consent, denied capability, runtime blocked, emergency stop, cleanup residue, retained provider homes/backups, post-delivery leak, and residual risk. Pair every irreversible or blocking action with “what happens,” “what remains,” and “how to recover.”
9. Extend §§11–12 with hostile repository/container, Compose-policy, network egress, production sentinel, sandbox secret replay/wrong-recipient/expiry, prompt-injection, emergency-stop, orphan reconciliation, and cleanup-residue fixtures across both agent paths and the required host/architecture matrix.

## Verdict

**revise.** It clears the bar for an evidence-integrity/redaction/package-security component specification, but misses the full-product P2 bar: hostile execution, egress and credential enforcement, safe dynamic-test authorization, prompt-injection defense, emergency stop/recovery, and severity/audit contracts must be explicit, testable, and usable before this can be frozen for implementation.
