## Scores

- **Requirements coverage — 8/10.** Sections 2–15 cover nearly every P2 topic, including malicious-source actors, prohibited actions, provider/hosted disclosure, credentials, redaction, incidents, audit, finding governance, and release tests; however, P2's required runtime-test safety classes, target-egress states, and residual-risk acceptance are not actually defined.
- **Assets, attacker model, and trust boundaries — 8.5/10.** Sections 2–3 explicitly include hostile repositories, dependencies, images, scanners, provider responses, local/LAN actors, operator error, decision-quality harm, and approved destinations as residual exfiltration channels, but the local UI/bootstrap boundary and provider-side/account-plan actors are not carried through to equally concrete controls.
- **Prohibited actions and authorization — 8/10.** Sections 6.1–6.2 bind approvals to destination, method, data, credential handle, expiry, and policy, and make production/destructive/broad-access actions non-waivable; the specification does not define how a non-specialist operator safely discovers and validates those technical fields or distinguish benign runtime session mutation from prohibited “state-changing” testing.
- **Container, nested-runtime, and source-isolation controls — 6.5/10.** Sections 5.1, 13.4, 13.5, 14.4, and gate 5 preserve provider separation, fixed analyzers, compiled Compose, Lima/rootless Docker, firewalling, and emergency teardown, but mostly restate architecture outcomes rather than reviewing exact worker profiles, acquisition controls, VM-internal control/data-plane ACLs, or the architecture's X25519/HKDF/AES-GCM secret-envelope construction.
- **Credential lifecycle and secret handling — 8/10.** Sections 5–8 and 12 define separate provider homes, SSH acquisition confinement, one-use sandbox handles, optional-service scoping, revocation, cleanup, and canary tests; the score is capped because persistent provider-home cleanup remains “explicit engagement cleanup,” rotation is external and untracked, and the frozen VM envelope profile is gated but not security-analyzed.
- **Network egress and hosted-service controls — 8.5/10.** Sections 6–7 and 14.3 give exact-destination approval, DNS/redirect/private-range defenses, byte bounds, revocation, no fallback, hostile-response quarantine, and route/credential separation; nevertheless, target runtime egress has no named state machine, and provider/Git/build channels are acknowledged residual channels without equivalent per-transfer approval and revocation semantics.
- **Safe dynamic testing and emergency control — 5.5/10.** Sections 6.2, 12, 13.5, 14.4, and gate 5 prohibit destructive/production activity and require emergency teardown, but no runtime-test class taxonomy, route/action allowlist, passive-versus-authenticated policy, request budget, login/session treatment, side-effect detection, origin binding, or per-control authorization contract exists.
- **Prompt injection and untrusted-content defense — 9/10.** Sections 4–5 and 14.1–14.2 provide a strong structural instruction hierarchy, no snapshot working directory, typed evidence access, five-command surface, proposal-only output, canaries, and laundering/replay fixtures across both providers; the residual dependency on provider CLI deny-read behavior is candidly gated rather than hidden.
- **Evidence integrity, privacy, redaction, and packaging — 8.5/10.** Sections 5.3, 8, 9, 13.4, 13.6, and 14.5–14.6 specify bounded admission, distinct evidence states, minimization, repeated redaction, screenshot re-encoding, safe rendering, deletion, package reopening, and seeded-secret tests; detailed evidence occurrence/supersession mechanics appropriately remain bound to the architecture.
- **Failure, incident response, and auditability — 7.5/10.** Sections 11–12 provide broad typed audit coverage, stop triggers/order, incident severity, fresh-session rules, reconciliation, and residue blocking, but stop/destroy/reconcile have no deadlines or acknowledgement/escalation states, and “resume only a demonstrably unaffected phase” leaves the proof and decision procedure undefined.
- **Finding governance and compliance-language safety — 8.5/10.** Sections 9–10 carefully separate technical severity, business priority, confidence, validation, coverage, applicability, and CVSS use; require fresh review for High/Critical findings; and deterministically reject unsupported compliance and rebuild claims. Reviewer qualification, disagreement resolution, and formal approval expiry are still underspecified.
- **Verification, operational usability, and buildability — 6.5/10.** Sections 13–15 assign controls to the frozen packages and define excellent adversarial suites, but the UI requirements are screen-level rather than a workable approval/incident/risk flow, runtime policy remains for P5 to invent, and release gates name residual risk without a schema, accountable authority, acceptance threshold, or expiry/review cycle.

## Overall

**7.8/10.** Equal-weight average. Contender 5 is the strongest product-wide safety contender in its provider/prompt-injection, disclosure, evidence-honesty, and abuse-test treatment. It remains below the P2 ship bar because the two decisions most likely to confront an operator—what dynamic behavior is safe to authorize and which residual risks may be accepted—are prose, not frozen contracts.

## Strongest objection

**Must-fix — the specification does not define runtime-test safety classes, so its non-waivable prohibition is not operationally implementable.** PLAN P2 explicitly requires “runtime-test safety classes” and target-egress states. Contender 5 instead says “destructive or state-changing security tests” have no approval path (§6.2, lines 303–320), while the architecture requires Playwright/passive probes and sandbox credentials. It never classifies passive observation, unauthenticated reads, authentication/session creation, safe fixture mutations, or prohibited actions; nor does it bind a control to origins/routes, methods, account/tenant, fixture IDs, credentials, budgets, expected side effects, and evidence/coverage consequences.

This ambiguity is not conservative in practice. Logging in creates sessions and may update last-login, lockout, audit, or queue state; even `GET` may mutate a hostile target. P5 must either treat all such activity as prohibited and miss required runtime coverage, or invent an authorization model during implementation. A hurried operator cannot tell what an approval permits or why a check is safe, and the release tests in §14 never test the missing policy. The classes and their transitions must be frozen before build.

## Other findings

1. **Must-fix — residual risks are disclosed, not accepted (§16, lines 843–869).** PLAN P2 requires residual-risk acceptance. The contender lists eight candid risks and requires them in documentation/packages, but defines no `ResidualRiskAcceptance` record, risk owner, affected assets/runs, likelihood/impact, compensating controls, acceptance authority, expiry/review date, revocation, customer acknowledgement, or release threshold. Gate 15 has no residual-risk gate. Consequently, a provider-auth exposure or approved-channel exfiltration risk can remain indefinitely “disclosed” with no accountable ship decision.

2. **Must-fix — operator approval is technically exact but practically unaided (§6.1 and §7).** A non-specialist must approve normalized scheme/host/port/path, methods, data categories, byte limits, credential handle, policy digest, retention terms, recipient, and residual risk. The spec does not say how these are discovered, populated from locked profiles, previewed, validated against the intended service/account, corrected, or reapproved after drift. This conflicts with the calm, guided UI decision and makes copy/paste or blanket approval likely.

3. **Must-fix — target egress has policy statements but no state machine (§§3, 6, 13.5, 14.3).** The architecture defines distinct build-acquisition, target-runtime, and internal-runtime network classes. The contender says runtime egress is denied and exact exceptions require approval, but does not freeze `OFF/PENDING/ACTIVE/REVOKING/REVOKED/FAILED`-equivalent states, packet cutoff semantics, in-flight behavior, retry/queue behavior, or how a changed resolution/certificate affects control results and coverage.

4. **Must-fix — the frozen VM secret-envelope profile is not actually reviewed (§13.5, §15.11).** Architecture §10.3 expressly says `safety.md` must review ephemeral X25519/HKDF-SHA-256/AES-256-GCM delivery. Contender 5 only requires one-use secrets and lists replay/recipient/expiry/cleanup as an “open architecture gate.” It does not validate key authenticity, entropy and nonce requirements, key/plaintext lifetime, crash dumps/swap, envelope size/versioning, associated-data canonicalization failure, or broker restart behavior.

5. **Must-fix — incident recovery has no bounded operational state machine (§12.2, lines 577–604).** “Bounded emergency path,” “preserve resource identity,” and “resume only a demonstrably unaffected phase” lack deadlines, acknowledgements, retry limits, escalation order, operator roles, proof criteria, and exact reducer states. If server/helper/broker is impaired, the UI may not be available to provide the promised actions. Reuse the architecture's deadline-bearing stop/emergency-stop/destroy/reconcile contracts and define residue outcomes.

6. **Must-fix — the provider boundary is strong, but static acquisition/analyzer containment is shallow (§13.4).** “Fixed adapters,” no shell/network/credentials, and isolated bounded parsing are useful, yet no normative UID, read-only root, snapshot/outbox mounts, seccomp/capability profile, output and decompression quotas, control-socket isolation, attempt/fence receipt binding, or cleanup/orphan behavior is specified. A malicious parser exploit is a named High threat (§3) but lacks a dedicated release fixture comparable to provider injection.

7. **Should-fix — local UI/session threat is named but not controlled in the safety spec (§2.1 and §13.2/13.7).** The contender refers to an “authenticated loopback UI,” exact origin, and single-purpose tokens, but does not require the architecture's one-use fragment bootstrap, HttpOnly/SameSite cookie attributes, CSRF/origin checks, bootstrap expiry, download authorization, or local-user file permissions. This is a direct gap between the local/LAN threat at §3 and its verification suite.

8. **Should-fix — residual-risk copy is not engagement-tailored by contract (§16).** “Engagement-appropriate form” has no deterministic source fields or review checks. A template could omit the approved destination, affected data categories, provider retention unknown, lack of provider-side deletion proof, or recipient/publication restriction while technically satisfying the prose requirement.

9. **Should-fix — reviewer independence is conceptually good but operationally incomplete (§9.2).** The spec distinguishes fresh-session perspective from organizational independence, but does not define reviewer eligibility, conflict handling, disagreement/escalation, review expiry after finding/evidence revision, or who can retain a Critical severity when reproduction is blocked.

10. **Should-fix — `SEV-0` notification guidance lacks a runbook owner and evidence-safe workflow (§12.3).** It says to notify engagement security/privacy owners and consult counsel without defining contact readiness, acknowledgement, sanitized incident export, authorization to transmit incident data, or what happens when no contact is configured. This could delay containment or leak the very data under investigation.

## Concrete fixes

1. Add a normative runtime-test authorization matrix with at least passive observation, unauthenticated read-only, sandbox-authenticated read-only, explicitly approved reversible fixture mutation if allowed by product scope, and prohibited production/destructive classes. Bind every control to target/runtime/image/control-plan digests, origin/routes/methods, account/tenant, fixture IDs, credential handles, budgets, side-effect sentinels, expiry, stop conditions, and coverage outcomes.
2. Define target-egress lifecycle states and receipts for build and runtime phases, including DNS/TLS pin validation, activation, expiry/revocation, in-flight cancellation, retry/queue prohibition, firewall acknowledgement, and post-cutoff packet proof.
3. Add a versioned residual-risk and acceptance schema with owner, scope, likelihood/impact, evidence, compensating controls, alternatives, customer disclosure, acceptance authority, expiry/review trigger, revocation, and package/release effect. Add a gate that blocks release on unaccepted, expired, or above-threshold risk.
4. Convert approvals into a guided operator flow: locked service profiles generate destinations/methods/categories; preflight resolves and verifies destination/account; preview shows exact sanitized payload and coverage delta; plain-language warnings explain consequences; mismatches yield actionable correction; material drift forces reapproval.
5. Review the architecture's VM envelope explicitly and add requirements/tests for key authenticity and lifetime, CSPRNG/nonces, canonical associated data, replay and wrong-recipient handling, crash/restart/swap/core-dump exposure, expiry races, zeroization evidence, and cryptographic agility/version rejection.
6. Specify deadline-bearing incident reducers using existing `vm.stop`, `vm.emergencyStop`, `vm.destroy`, status, and reconciliation contracts. Define acknowledgement, retry/escalation, read-only fallback instructions when the server is unavailable, residue classification, proof for “unaffected phase,” and allowed operator dispositions.
7. Add exact acquisition/static-worker isolation profiles and adversarial fixtures for parser compromise, cross-run reads, helper/socket access, output floods, bombs, stale-fence receipts, process orphaning, and cleanup residue.
8. Freeze UI/session controls and operator runbooks for bootstrap, approval, download, deletion, incident contacts, credential rotation evidence, package quarantine, and engagement-specific residual-risk review.

## Verdict

**Revise.** It clears the bar for prompt-injection containment, hosted-service disclosure, evidence/privacy governance, and adversarial verification, and it is a strong synthesis base. It misses the P2 ship bar because runtime authorization, target-egress lifecycle, accountable residual-risk acceptance, and bounded operator recovery remain undefined and would otherwise be invented during P5.
