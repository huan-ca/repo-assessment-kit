## Scores

- **Requirements coverage — 8.0/10.** The contender covers almost every P2 domain with unusually concrete controls: hostile source and parsers (§§2, 5–6), provider/SSH/sandbox credentials (§§4–5, 7–8), nested runtime and Compose (§§8–11), egress (§10), evidence and packaging (§12), emergency stop (§13), release tests (§§14–15), and residual risk (§16). It still omits three things P2 explicitly requires as specification content: a runtime-test safety-class model, a normative audit-event contract, and security-finding severity/confidence/validation rules.
- **Assets, attacker model, and trust boundaries — 9.5/10.** Sections 2–4 identify people, source, credentials, provider, helper, broker, daemon, browser, analyzer, evidence, package, and other-run assets, then connect them to realistic malicious-repository, compromised-tool, replay, resource-exhaustion, and exfiltration stories. The main remaining blind spot is the same-host local-user/browser-session adversary around persistent provider homes and the loopback UI.
- **Prohibited actions and authorization — 8.5/10.** The ship posture and SI-01 through SI-10 clearly prohibit host Docker authority, generic privileged operations, broad mounts, production credentials, arbitrary egress, direct Compose execution, and silent weakening. Dynamic authorization is less complete: §10.2 says mutating methods require a separately approved flow, but does not define the allowable safety classes, side-effect budgets, cleanup proof, or control-plan fields needed to distinguish a safe login POST from a prohibited state change.
- **Container, nested-runtime, and source-isolation controls — 9.5/10.** Sections 5–6 and 8–11 give a complete defense-in-depth path from immutable acquisition through isolated analyzers to a mount-free Lima VM, rootless broker-only Docker, regenerated Compose plans, non-root/read-only services, default-deny networking, resource ceilings, and residue-aware teardown. The controls are also tied to adversarial fixtures rather than merely asserted.
- **Credential lifecycle and secret handling — 8.5/10.** The data map, one-acquisition-worker SSH design, engagement-separated provider homes, recipient-bound one-use sandbox credentials, X25519/HKDF/AES-GCM envelope, expiry/replay rules, tmpfs delivery, revocation, and cleanup are strong. Operationally, the spec does not freeze the actual provider-token delivery primitive for the supported environment-based CLI modes in `DECISIONS.md`, and retained provider homes have no engagement-close or inactivity cleanup default.
- **Network egress and hosted-service controls — 8.5/10.** Sections 4, 7, and 10 separate network identities, deny generic internet, block private/LAN/metadata and bypass paths, require explicit optional-service disclosure, and candidly disclose approved-destination exfiltration. A material enforceability gap remains in §10.1: the proxy is expected to honor optional HTTPS path prefixes and methods, but the spec does not define TLS termination, a trusted CA, or another mechanism by which it can observe encrypted package-manager requests; destination and byte controls are enforceable, path/method controls may not be.
- **Safe dynamic testing and emergency control — 7.0/10.** Capability blocking, production-like endpoint denial, recipient-bound test credentials, disposable probes, method restrictions, hard resource limits, host-owned emergency power-off, and the §13 stop sequence are strong. P2 nevertheless requires runtime-test safety classes, and the contender never freezes passive, authenticated read-only, reversible sandbox mutation, and prohibited classes or binds planned controls to origins, actions, data, accounts, state-change budgets, cleanup, and evidence.
- **Prompt injection and untrusted-content defense — 9.0/10.** Section 7 combines instruction precedence with a narrow evidence broker, fixed operations, no model authority over approvals/lifecycle/runtime/packaging, compartment and egress controls, proposal-only output, fresh review, and dual-provider adversarial fixtures. It also honestly acknowledges that a compromised provider CLI still holds its own credential while processing hostile context.
- **Evidence integrity, privacy, redaction, and packaging — 9.5/10.** Sections 3–4 and 12 provide one-way receipt movement, fenced admission, attachment-only hostile formats, typed report ASTs, metadata-stripped derived previews, allowlisted logs, two-stage secret scanning, immutable staging, fresh-process ZIP validation, and honest checksum semantics. The one minor workflow gap is that a “human-reviewed structured redaction rule” and “required independent/human review” have no per-run reviewer eligibility, decision record, or delta-invalidation contract.
- **Failure, incident response, and auditability — 7.0/10.** Sections 11 and 13 have excellent fail-closed behavior, externally enforced emergency stop, bounded escalation, exact-resource cleanup, no unsafe guest collection, operator notification, and no automatic resume. But §13.2 invents `SECURITY_HOLD`, which does not exist in frozen architecture §6.1, and “audit streams”/records are prose rather than a versioned event vocabulary with emission points, required fields, correlation, retention, and export policy.
- **Finding governance and compliance-language safety — 5.5/10.** Section 4 correctly limits applicability and compliance claims, but the contender never defines how technical severity, business priority, confidence, CVSS sufficiency, validation states, disputes, supersession, independent reproduction, or release effects are assigned. References to “Critical/High” release blockers therefore have no deterministic policy behind them.
- **Verification, ownership, and parallel buildability — 8.5/10.** Section 14 is an exceptional negative-test inventory across acquisition, analyzers, both providers, four native host/architecture combinations, Compose, escape, egress, DoS, secrets, active content, packaging, crash recovery, and blocked-runtime outcomes. Buildability is capped because P4/P5/P6 would have to reconcile the undefined incident state/events and invent dynamic authorization and finding-governance contracts independently.
- **Operational usability — 6.5/10.** Static-only success, precise blocked reasons, explicit attempted safe steps, containment notifications, no-recall language, and a clean “start a new fenced run” recovery posture are good operator outcomes. Normal and exceptional workflows are still incomplete: there is no implementable UI/API state for `SECURITY_HOLD`, no provider-refusal outcome, no grouping/diff model for exact destination approvals, no bounded reviewer workflow, and no accountable residual-risk acceptance record.
- **Residual-risk treatment — 8.5/10.** Section 16 is candid about hypervisor/kernel escape, compromised trusted dependencies, provider retention, approved-destination exfiltration, in-VM lateral compromise, checksum limits, imperfect redaction, static-only limits, and physical-host assumptions. It does not say who may accept each residual risk, for which run/control, with what compensating controls, expiry, customer disclosure, and re-review trigger; indefinite provider-home/package retention and local-session compromise are also understated.

## Overall

**8.1/10 (equal-weight average).** This is the most complete kind of safety contender P2 needs: hostile-source assumptions are carried through executable controls and release fixtures. It is not freeze-ready because its incident lifecycle contradicts the already-frozen architecture, while runtime authorization, audit events, and finding governance remain underspecified.

## Strongest objection

**Must-fix — the incident workflow is operationally unusable because it depends on a run state forbidden by the frozen contract.** Section 13.2 says to “atomically mark the run `SECURITY_HOLD`,” fence work, reject admission/package/download, and prohibit automatic resume. Section 15.2 then makes an “active security hold” a package blocker. Frozen architecture §6.1 defines a closed `RunState` transition graph, makes `COMPLETED`, `CANCELLED`, and `FAILED` terminal, and rejects any illegal transition with `409 RUN_STATE_CONFLICT`; it has no `SECURITY_HOLD`. Architecture §8.8’s closed `RunEvent.type` union also has no incident, credential-revocation, egress-cut, residue, or recovery event.

This is the highest-rework defect because the missing state is not just terminology. P4/P5/P6 must independently implement persistence, compare-and-swap transitions, restart reconciliation, HTTP operations, SSE events, UI status/actions, package eligibility, and successor-run behavior. As written, they must either violate the safety spec at the moment of a suspected compromise or reopen the frozen architecture ad hoc, making containment least reliable precisely when operators need it most.

## Other findings

1. **Must-fix — runtime-test safety classes are absent (§10.2 and §§14.3–15).** “Mutating HTTP methods are denied unless the exact non-production flow is separately approved” is not an authorization model. The spec does not classify passive observation, unauthenticated/read-only access, authenticated read, reversible sandbox mutation, exploit validation, or prohibited behavior, nor bind each class to origin, methods, accounts, synthetic data, side-effect budget, cleanup, rate, evidence, and failure status. This directly misses P2’s required content and risks divergent Codex/Claude behavior.
2. **Must-fix — finding governance is not defined (entire spec; especially §§14–15).** The contender uses Critical/High as release-significant concepts without defining severity assignment, business priority separation, confidence, validation-state transitions, CVSS prerequisites, imported-tool findings, disputes, supersession, reviewer independence, or acceptance authority. Architecture supplies fields, not the safety policy governing them.
3. **Must-fix — auditability is descriptive, not contractual (§§3, 8, 10, 13).** “Audit streams,” proxy log fields, and incident records do not form a closed, schema-versioned event catalogue. There are no mandatory emission points for approval grant/revoke, provider dispatch, secret issue/redeem/reject, policy denial, egress decision, helper/broker anomaly, security stop, residue, finding review, evidence admission/rejection, or package release, and no actor/correlation/fence fields or retention/export rules.
4. **Must-fix — HTTPS path/method enforcement is not implementable as specified (§10.1).** Package managers and registries use TLS. An external proxy can enforce destination, port, IP, timing, and coarse bytes, but cannot enforce HTTP path and method without terminating TLS or using a cooperating application gateway. The contender specifies neither a protected interception CA nor client compatibility, certificate validation, streaming behavior, and real-provider/package-manager tests. Approval promises therefore exceed the proven enforcement boundary.
5. **Should-fix — operator approval can become high-friction and error-prone (§§4, 10).** Exact destination/path/method/data/credential/run approvals are defensible, but the spec provides no signed release-owned destination bundles, discovery/preview step, grouped consent, expiry warning, policy diff, or “deny and continue with reduced coverage” summary. Repeated opaque prompts will encourage habitual approval and weaken the control in practice.
6. **Should-fix — provider refusal has no defined safe outcome (§§4 and 7).** Provider inference is disclosed as an external transfer and appears core to assessment, but the spec does not say whether refusing it blocks the whole run, permits a deterministic scanner-only package, or creates another supported reduced-coverage path. Operators cannot predict the consequence of the most important data-sharing decision.
7. **Should-fix — independent and human review are release gates without a workflow (§§7, 12, 15).** The contender names a fresh-session reviewer, an independent security reviewer, and required human review, but does not define reviewer eligibility, independence proof, queue/inventory, input digest, accept/object decisions, disagreement handling, timeout/unavailability, delta invalidation, or the operator-visible blocked state.
8. **Should-fix — residual-risk acceptance is disclosure-only (§16).** P2 requires residual-risk acceptance, but the contender specifies only documentation. There is no durable record of approver, affected run/control, rationale, compensating controls, expiry, customer-visible wording, or trigger for re-review.
9. **Should-fix — retention relies on operator memory (§4).** Provider homes and packages persist until explicit cleanup; admitted evidence and snapshots live for the run. Add an engagement-close inventory, inactivity reminder, deliberate renewal, and cleanup outcome so reusable provider credentials and customer data do not remain indefinitely by omission.
10. **Nice-to-have — the local control-plane threat model should cover same-host attacks (§§2.2, 12).** The session cookie, loopback bind, Origin check, and CSRF token are good, but no release fixture covers bootstrap/session replay, localhost DNS rebinding, malicious local processes, stale downloads, or retained provider-home access by another host user.

## Concrete fixes

1. Reconcile incident containment with P1 before freezing P2: either version the architecture to add `SECURITY_HOLD` plus legal transitions, DB schema, idempotent incident/recovery APIs, typed SSE/audit events, package/download restrictions, restart behavior, and successor-run semantics, or map the same guarantees onto existing canonical states and a separately typed durable incident disposition.
2. Add a versioned runtime-control authorization matrix with at least `passive-observation`, `read-only`, `authenticated-read`, `reversible-sandbox-mutation`, and `prohibited`; bind every planned control to exact origin, methods/actions, account and secret purpose, synthetic data, side-effect/rate budget, cleanup proof, evidence, cancellation behavior, and coverage outcome.
3. Freeze finding governance: technical severity, business priority, exploitability, confidence, validation states and legal transitions, CVSS sufficiency, imported findings, disputes/invalidation/supersession, independent-review rules, and release/residual-acceptance effects.
4. Define a closed security audit-event schema with actor/workload, run/attempt/fence, authority or policy digest, safe allowlisted fields, correlation and ordering, retention, package visibility, and mandatory events for approvals, egress, secrets, provider tasks, helper/broker operations, denials, stops, residue, evidence, review, and release.
5. Clarify the build-egress enforcement boundary. Either specify and test a protected TLS-terminating gateway compatible with every supported client, reduce consent to destination/port/byte controls that can actually be enforced, or move dependency resolution into a release-owned fetch step followed by physically networkless target-controlled build execution.
6. Add operator workflow contracts for provider refusal, destination-bundle discovery and diffs, grouped consent/revocation, emergency-stop acknowledgement, incident triage, residue, reviewer queues, and successor-run creation. Every blocked state should show what ran, what did not, what remains at risk, and the safe next actions.
7. Add a durable residual-risk acceptance record containing approver role, run/control scope, rationale, compensating controls, expiry, customer-visible limitation, and re-review trigger; make unaccepted release-significant residuals block package release.
8. Add engagement-close retention controls and local-control-plane adversarial fixtures, including session/bootstrap replay, DNS rebinding, cross-origin mutations, another local-user/process probe, stale download authorization, and provider-home cleanup verification.

## Verdict

**revise.** The contender clears the bar for hostile-repository isolation, credential intent, prompt-injection containment, evidence/package safety, emergency stop design, and adversarial verification. It misses the freeze bar because the incident state cannot be represented by the frozen architecture and P2-mandated dynamic authorization, audit, finding-governance, and residual-acceptance workflows are not yet complete enough for independent implementation lanes.
